# The most boring book in your building

Nobody reads the fire log book for fun. But your fire risk assessor reads it, inspectors ask for it, and after an incident it's one of the first things anyone looks at. It's how you show your fire alarm is maintained, as the Fire Safety Order requires.

Keep it boring: complete, up to date and easy to find.

## The weekly test

This is the entry people check first. Each week, test a different call point, so every one gets used in turn. Write down:

- the date and time
- which call point
- who did the test
- whether the sounders worked
- any faults

It takes two minutes a week.

## Service visits

After every visit, the log should show the date, who carried it out, what was tested, what was found and fixed, anything replaced, whether the system was left working, and the certificate reference.

## Faults, false alarms and real alarms

Each one gets its own entry: when it happened, what happened, which device, what caused it, what was done about it, and whether the fire service came. Be specific. "Alarm went off, reset" helps nobody. "Detector 14, first-floor kitchen, steam from the dishwasher, reset 10:42" helps everyone.

## Changes to the system

Moving a detector a metre counts as a change. So does adding one, taking one out or changing what the alarm switches off. Record every change, and have the system drawings updated to match.

## At the front of the book

- the building's name and address
- the Responsible Person
- who maintains the system
- the system's category under BS 5839-1
- when it was first commissioned

## Where it lives

On site, by the panel, where the people who need it can find it. The book belongs to the building, not to whoever services it.
